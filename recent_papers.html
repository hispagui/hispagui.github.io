Latest breakthrough in the Inverse Galois Problem

The paper that I want to highlight today settles a question that had been open for almost four decades: it shows that the Mathieu group \(M_{23}\), the last remaining sporadic group, is a Galois group over \(\mathbb{Q}\). It is titled The Mathieu group \(M_{23}\) is a Galois group over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and is freely available at arXiv 2608.08538. In my opinion, it is also a compelling example for how LLMs can assist researchers: the authors used AI tools for litterature research, code generation and testing hypotheses, while writing the paper themsevlves and verifying the final results without AI.

A bit of context first. In Galois theory, we can attach to any polynomial with rational coefficients a finite group \(G\), its Galois group. If you don't know what Galois groups are, you can think of them as objects that record symmetries along a specific polynomial's roots (this is very informal, the subtelty here is that the Galois group should also preserve algebraic relations over the base field).

Now, the Inverse Galois Problem turns this arround and asks; given a finite group \(G\), is there a polynomial with rational coefficients whose Galois group is \(G\)? Comuting Galois groups of a given polynomial is a well-understood (if sometimes laborious) task, but the converse is very very very hard. We don't even know whether every finite group can be realised thsi way, and the question remains very much open.

However, much progress has been made. We know, for instance, that every symmetric, alternating groups and solvable groups (so all abelian groups as well), occurs as Galois groups over \(\mathbb{Q}\). Another natural place to test the problem is the finite simple groups. They can be though of as the building blocks from which all finite groups are assembled (they are really helpful for understanding the structures of certain finite groups). They have been extensively studied since you can be understand them as (they are quite important). There famous classification, one of the longest proofs in mathematics, says that every finite simple group is either a cyclic group of prime order, an alternating group, a group of Lie type or one of the 26 exceptional sporadic groups.

Today's paper focuses on one of those sporadic groups. Each of them is unusual in its own way, and some are enormous: the largest, caled the Monster, has about \(8 \times 10^{53}\) elements. In the 1980's, mathematicians showed that 25 of them occur as Galois groups over \(\mathbb{Q}\), using what we call the rigidity method (and its variants). Surprisingly (or not), the method builds on rich geometric mathematics: Riemann surfaces, monodromy, fundamental groups, descent theory and modulli spaces.

The trick is that over the field \( \mathbb{C}(t) \), every finite group DOES occur as a Galois group, thanks to the topology of the punctured Riemann sphere. The rigidity method gives a criterion under which such a realisation can be "descended" from \(\mathbb{C}(t)\) to \(\mathbb{Q}(t)\). Then, by substituting suitable rational numbers for \(t\), one obtains polynomials over \(\mathbb{Q}\) with the desired Galois group. \(M_{23}\) was the one sporadic group for which this approach kept failling.

For mathematicians reading this:

First of all, the field of meromorphic functions of the Riemann sphere is isomorphic to the function field \(\mathbb{C}(t) \cong \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}))\), and let \(S = \{ P_1, ..., P_r \}\) be a finite set of points on \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\). The two main results are the following equivalences of categories:
```math \{ \text{Compact connected Riemann surfaces } X \text{ with a non-constant map} X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) \} ``` being equivalent to ```math \{ \text{Finite field extensions of} \mathbb{C}(t) \} ``` And the mathching, from covering-space theory between covers and subgroups up to conjugacy, and finite covers with finite-index subgroups. More precisely: A finite connected covering \(X^{\circ}\) of \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S\) corresponds to a subgroup \(H \le \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\). A finite normal covering \(Z^{\circ}\) corresponds to a normal subgroup \(N \trianglelefteq \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\). And the quotient group \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)/N \cong Aut(Z|\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\), is the group of deck transformations for \(N \leftrightarrow Z\).

These two worlds (equivalences) are linked by filling in the punctures. Near each point \(P_i\) in \(S\), a finite covering of \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S\) is a disjoint union of punctured discs (represented by \(z \mapsto z^e\) in complex analysis). Now, filling them in gives a compact Riemann surface \(X = \widebar{X^{circ}}\) with a branched covering \(X \to \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\), and the deck transformations extend. (Since \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\) is simply connected, it is the branching over \(S\) that makes non-trivial covers possible.) The last piece of information we need is that, the topology of Riemann surfaces lets any finite group \(G\) be realised as a quotient of the fundamental group of the Riemann sphere minus finitely many points \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S )/ N \).

Finally, we have all the ingredients. Take a finite group \(G\), it's isomorphic to a quotient \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})- S) / N\), for \(S\) a finite number of points and \(N\) the normal subgroup corresponding to the finite unramified Galois cover \(X^{\circ}\) over \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S \) (it is a \(\sharp G\) sheeted cover). This Galois unramified cover \(X^{\circ}\) corresponds (by filling in) to a compact Riemann surface \(X\) with a branched \(\sharp G\)-sheeted Galois covering \(X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\), (the branching is what makes our cover non-trivial). And this branched cover corresponds to a finite Galois extension \(\mathcal{M}(X) | \mathbb{C}(t) \) of degree \(\sharp G\). And to tie everything together, the Galois group of this finite Galois extensions \(Gal(\mathcal{M}(X) | \mathbb{C}(t)) \cong Aut(X | \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})) \cong \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})- S) / N \) is exactly \(G\).

The only holdout was the Mathieu group \(M_{23}\). Well, we now have a proof \(4\) decades later! The issue was that to use the rigidity method, one needs to find a specific rigid triple of conjugacy classes. And for \( M_{23} \) such conjugacy classes cannot be rigid.
