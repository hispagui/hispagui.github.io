Latest breakthrough in the Inverse Galois Problem

The paper that I want to highlight today settles a question that had been open for almost four decades: it shows that the Mathieu group \(M_{23}\), the last remaining sporadic group, is a Galois group over \(\mathbb{Q}\).
It is titled The Mathieu group \(M_{23}\) is a Galois group over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and is freely available at arXiv 2608.08538. In my opinion, it is also a compelling example for how LLMs can assist researchers: the authors used AI tools for litterature research, code generation and testing hypotheses, while writing the paper themsevlves and verifying the final results without AI.

A bit of context first. In Galois theory, we can attach to any polynomial with rational coefficients a finite group \(G\), its Galois group. If you don't know what Galois groups are, you can think of them as algebraic objects that record symmetries along a specific polynomial's roots (this is quite informal, the subtelty here is that the Galois group should also preserve algebraic relations over the base field). Now, the Inverse Galois Problem turns this arround and asks; given a finite group \(G\), is there a polynomial with rational coefficients whose Galois group is \(G\)? Comuting Galois groups of a given polynomial is a well-understood (if sometimes laborious) task, but the converse is very very very hard. We don't even know whether every finite group can be realised thsi way, and the question remains very much open.

However, much progress has been made. We know for instance, that every symmetric, alternating groups and solvable groups (so all abelian groups as well), occur as Galois groups over \(\mathbb{Q}\). Another natural place to test the problem is the finite simple groups. They can be though of as the building blocks from which all finite groups are assembled (they are really helpful for understanding the structures of most finite groups). Their famous classification, one of the longest proofs in mathematics, says that every finite simple group is either a cyclic group of prime order, an alternating group, a group of Lie type or one of the 26 exceptional sporadic groups.

Today's paper focuses on one of those sporadic groups. Each of them is unusual in its own way, and some are enormous, the largest, caled the Monster, has about \(8 \times 10^{53}\) elements. In the 1980's, mathematicians showed that 25 of them occur as Galois groups over \(\mathbb{Q}\), using what we call the rigidity method (and variations of it). Surprisingly (or not), the method builds on rich geometric mathematics: Riemann surfaces, monodromy, fundamental groups, descent theory and modulli spaces.

The trick is that over the field \( \mathbb{C}(t) \), every finite group DOES occur as a Galois group, thanks to the topology of the punctured Riemann sphere. The rigidity method gives a criterion under which such a realisation can be "descended" from \(\mathbb{C}(t)\) to \(\mathbb{Q}(t)\). Then, by substituting suitable rational numbers for \(t\), one obtains polynomials over \(\mathbb{Q}\) with the desired Galois group. \(M_{23}\) was the one sporadic group for which this approach kept failling.

This is the sort of result I love in mathematics! It is a problem of algebra and combinatorics (realising finite groups as Galois groups), and we end up using geometry, topology and complex analysis to say whehter this is possible or not!

And, for mathematicians reading this:

So, over \(\mathbb{C}(t)\), every finite group occurs as the Galois group of some polynomial. The first reason for this is that the field of meromorphic functions of the Riemann sphere is isomorphic to the function field \(\mathbb{C}(t) (\cong \mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}))\)), and let \(S = \{ P_1, ..., P_r \}\) be a finite set of points on \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\). Next, the main reason for this are the following equivalences of categories: {Compact connected Riemann surfaces \(X\) with a non-constant map \(X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\)}
is equivalent to {Finite field extensions of \(\mathbb{C}(t)\)}.
And the mathching (actually an equivalence of categories), from covering-space theory between covers and subgroups up to conjugacy, and finite covers with finite-index subgroups. More precisely: A finite connected covering \(X^{\circ}\) of \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S\) corresponds to a subgroup \(H \le \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\). A finite normal covering \(Z^{\circ}\) corresponds to a normal subgroup \(N \trianglelefteq \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\). And the quotient group \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)/N \cong Aut(Z|\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\), is the group of deck transformations for \(N \leftrightarrow Z\).

These two worlds (equivalences) are linked by filling in the punctures. Near each point \(P_i\) in \(S\), a finite covering of \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S\) is a disjoint union of punctured discs (represented by \(z \mapsto z^e\) in complex analysis). Now, filling them in gives a compact Riemann surface \(X = \overline{X^{\circ}}\) with a branched covering \(X \to \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\), and the deck transformations extend. (Since \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\) is simply connected, it is the branching over \(S\) that makes non-trivial covers possible.) The last piece of information we need is that, the topology of Riemann surfaces lets any finite group \(G\) be realised as a quotient of the fundamental group of the Riemann sphere minus finitely many points \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S )/ N \).

Finally, we have all the ingredients. Take a finite group \(G\), it's isomorphic to a quotient \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})- S) / N\), for \(S\) a finite number of points and \(N\) the normal subgroup corresponding to the finite unramified Galois cover \(X^{\circ}\) over \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S \) (it is a \(\sharp G\) sheeted cover). This Galois unramified cover \(X^{\circ}\) corresponds (by filling in) to a compact Riemann surface \(X\) with a branched \(\sharp G\)-sheeted Galois covering \(X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\), (the branching is what makes our cover non-trivial). And this branched cover corresponds to a finite Galois extension \(\mathcal{M}(X) | \mathbb{C}(t) \) of degree \(\sharp G\). And to tie everything together, the Galois group of this finite Galois extensions \(Gal(\mathcal{M}(X) | \mathbb{C}(t)) \cong Aut(X | \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})) \cong \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})- S) / N \) is exactly \(G\).

I will not attempt to explain how the rigidity method works in detail.
But the obstacle for the Mathieu group \(M_{23}\) was the following. To apply the rigidity method, one needs to find a specific triple of conjugacy classes: stable under absolute Galois group \(G_{\mathbb{Q}}\) action and with Nielsen class of size one. But for every triple of conjugacy classes of \(M_{23}\) that is stable under \(G_{\mathbb{Q}}\), the Nielsen class never has exactly one element. What the authors did was take the \(G_{\mathbb{Q}}\)-stable triple, with the smallest non-zero Nielsen class size, in this case 7. By Riemann's existence theorem, each of these 7 elements correspond to an \(M_{23}\)-cover of the Riemann sphere (and they are algebraic). Since the triple is \(G_{\mathbb{Q}}\)-stable, \(G_{\mathbb{Q}}\) permutes the 7 covers. But the more important question is how it permutes them? This cannot be answered by simply looking at the group structure, the authors had to compute these covers explicitly, then verify how they were permutted. The "miracle" they found is that, \(G_{\mathbb{Q}}\) has a fixed point, one of the seven covers is send to itself (up to iso.)! From there, the argument follows the last steps of the rigidity method. And they obtain a regular Galois extension of \(\mathbb{Q}(t)\) whose Galois group is \(M_{23}\). Finally, choosing a suitable rational substitue for \(t\) and applying Hilbert's irreducibility theorem, they proved that the last Mathieu group, \(M_{23}\) was realisable as the Galois group of the field of rationals.

In the end, the argument traced back to the rigidity method, only in the other cases, the single Nielsen class automatically gives a fixed point, to work with.
