Latest breakthrough in the Inverse Galois Problem

The paper that I want to highlight today made an outstanding novel contributions to mathematics. In my opinion, it is also serves as a compelling example for how LLMs can be used as valuable tools for assisting researchers in their work. It is titled: the Mathieu group \(M_{23}\) is a Galois group over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and is freely available at arXiv 2608.08538.

A bit of context first; in Galois theory, we can attach to any polynomial with rational coefficients (\(\mathbb{Q})\) a finite group \(G\), its Galois group. If you don't know what Galois groups are, you can think of them as groups that record symmetries along a specific polynomial's roots (this is very informal, the subtelty here is that the Galois group should also preserve algebraic relations over the base field).

Now, the Inverse Galois Problem turns this arround and asks; given a finite group \(G\), is there a polynomial with coefficients in \(\mathbb{Q}\) whose Galois group is the same as \(G\)? While it is not hard to find the Galois group of any polynomial over \(\mathbb{Q}\), the converse is very very very hard in general. We don't even know if it possible to realise every finite group as Galois groups associated to a certain polynomial.
Therefore this question remains open.

However, some progress has been made. For example, we know that for alternating groups, abelian groups and solvable groups, it is always possible to find polynomials such that their Galois groups are one of these. Another natural place to test the problem is for a special set of groups called finite simple groups. They have been extensively studied since you can be understand them as the building blocks from which all finite groups are assembled (they are quite important). There is even a famous classification of finite simple groups (also the longest mathematical proof in history!), saying that each finite simple group belongs to a few infinite families: cyclic groups of prime order, alternating groups, groups of Lie type or sporadic groups.

Today's paper focuses on one of those sporadic groups. They are a total of \(26\) sporadic groups, each of them being different in their own way and they usually have high order (one of them even has approximately \(8 \times 10^{53}\) elements). The cool thing about these sporadic groups is that for \(25\) of them, mathematicians in the \(80\)'s have shown that they occur as Galois groups of certain polynomials. They used a method called the rigidity method (and its variants) to prove this. Surprisingly (or not), the method combines complexes geometric areas of mathematics such as Riemann surfaces, monodromy, fundamental groups, descent theory and modulli spaces.
The trick is that over the field \( \mathbb{C}(t) \), every finite group DOES occur as the Galois group of a finite Galois extension of \(\mathbb{C}(t)\). Now the whole idea of the rigidity method is to "descend" this kind of relation over to \(\mathbb{Q}\).

For mathematicians reading this:

First of all, the field of meromorphic functions of the Riemann sphere \(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})) \cong \mathbb{C}(t)\), and let \(S = \{ P_1, ..., P_r \}\) be a finite set of points on \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\). The main results here is the equivalence of categories between
\( \{ \) Compact connected Riemann surfaces \(X\) with a non-constant map \(X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\} \) and
\(\{\) Finite field extensions of \(\mathbb{C}(t) \} \). And the mathching, from covering-space theory between covers and subgroups up to conjugacy, and finite covers and finite-index subgroups. More precisely:
A finite connected covering \(X\) of \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S\) corresponds to a subgroup \(H \le \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\). A finite normal covering \(Z\) corresponds to a normal subgroup \(N \trianglelefteq \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\). And the quotient group \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)/N \cong Aut(Z|\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S)\) the group of deck transformations for \(N \leftrightarrow Z\).
These two worlds are linked by filling in the punctures. Near each \(P_i\), a finite covering of \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S\) is a disjoint union of punctured discs \(z \mapsto z^e\). Now, filling them in gives a compact Riemann surface \(X\) with a branched covering \(X \to \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}),\) and the deck transformations extend. (Since \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\) is simply connected, it is the branching over \(S\) that makes non-trivial covers possible.)
The last piece of information we need is that, the topology of Riemann surfaces lets any finite group \(G\) be realised as a quotient of the fundamental group of the Riemann sphere minus finitely many points, \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S )/ N \), this may sound familiar!
Finally, we have all the ingredients. Take a finite group \(G\), it's isomorphic to a quotient \(\pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})- S) / N\), for \(S\) a finite number of points and \(N\) the normal subgroup corresponding to the finite unramified Galois cover \(X^{\circ}\) over \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C}) - S \) (it is a \(\sharp G\) sheeted cover). This Galois unramified cover \(X^{\circ}\) corresponds (by filling in) to a compact Riemann surface \(X\) with a branched \(\sharp G\)-sheeted Galois covering \(X \rightarrow \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})\), (the branching is what makes our cover non-trivial). And this branched cover corresponds to a finite Galois extension \(\mathcal{M}(X) | \mathbb{C}(t) \) of degree \(\sharp G\). And to tie everything together, the Galois group of this finite Galois extensions \(Gal(\mathcal{M}(X) | \mathbb{C}(t)) \cong Aut(X | \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})) \cong \pi_1(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{C})- S) / N \) is exactly \(G\).

The only holdout was the Mathieu group \(M_{23}\). Well, we now have a proof \(4\) decades later! The issue was that to use the rigidity method, one needs to find a specific rigid triple of conjugacy classes. And for \( M_{23} \) such conjugacy classes cannot be rigid.
