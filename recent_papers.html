A small breakthrough for the Inverse Galois Problem

The paper that I want to highlight today made an outstanding novel contributions to mathematics and in my opinion also serves as a compelling example of how LLMs can serve as valuable tools for assisting researchers in their work. It is titled: the Mathieu group \(M_{23}\) is a Galois group over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and is freely available at arXiv 2608.08538.

A bit of context first; in Galois theory, we can attach to any polynomial with rational coefficients (\(\mathbb{Q})\) a finite group \(G\), its Galois group. If you don't know what Galois groups are, you can think of them as groups that record symmetries along a specific polynomial's roots (this is very informal, the subtelty here is that the Galois group should also preserve algebraic relations over the base field).
Now, the Inverse Galois Problem turns this arround and asks; given a finite group \(G\), is there a polynomial with coefficients in \(\mathbb{Q}\) whose Galois group is the same as \(G\)?
While it is not hard to find the Galois group of any polynomial over \(\mathbb{Q}\), the converse is very very very hard in general. We don't even know if it possible to realise every finite group as Galois groups associated to a certain polynomial.
Therefore this question remains open.

However, some progress has been made. For example, we know that for alternating groups, abelian groups and solvable groups, it is always possible to find polynomials such that their Galois groups are one of these. Another natural place to test the problem is for a special set of groups called finite simple groups. They have been extensively studied as they can be understood as the building blocks from which all finite groups are assembled (they are quite important). There is even a famous classification of finite simple groups (also the longest mathematical proof in history!), saying that each finite simple group belongs to a few infinite families:
cyclic groups of prime order, alternating groups, groups of Lie type or sporadic groups.

Today we are interested in those sporadic groups. They are a total of \(26\) sporadic groups, each of them being different in their own way and they usually have high order (one of them even has approximately \(8 \times 10^{53}\) elements). The cool thing about these sporadic groups is that for \(25\) of them, mathematicians in the \(80\)'s have shown that they occur as Galois groups of certain polynomials. They used a method called the rigidity method (and its variants). The method combines complexes geometric areas of mathematics such as Riemann surfaces, monodromy, fundamental groups, descent theory and modulli spaces.
