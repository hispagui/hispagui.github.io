A peculiar interpretation of derivatives

The motivation for this article comes from William Thurston's superb essay On proof and progress in mathematics. In this discussion I will explore deep and intricate connections between mathematical concetps that are already hard on their own. My goal is to provide the best possible intuition for these ideas. To that end, I will resist the urge to using technical language and do my best to keep the discussion light and visual. However, for sake of understanding, I must introduce two important abstract concepts: differential forms and (co)homology.

As the title suggests, this essay is about derivatives. You have certainly encountered derivatives before, and you may even be very familiar with them. However, there is an important caveat. You may know how to differentiate functions that live in Euclidean space, but how do you differentiate a function defined on, say, a Klein bottle (a surface with no inside, no outside and no boundary) ? This might be strange at first, but the question is very important! Mathematicians and physicists work with curved, hyperbolic, ..., all sorts of weird spaces all the time!
For example, imagine you want to know the speed of a probe that you have sent into space. Well this probe now lives in a curved space (spacetime) and you have to take the curvature of spacetime into account in your calcultions.

The question then becomes: how do we do calculus on weird spaces ? How can we make sense of a "rate of change", a "flow" or an "area" when there is no nice grid of straight lines to rely on? A big part of the answer is differential forms! Essentially, they tools are used to measure something geometric: how fast a quantity changes along a direction, how much of a flow passes through a surface, and so on Let me show you concretely what I mean.

Take a function, say \(f(x,y) = x^2+y^2\), and think of it as the altitude of a landscape above the point \((x,y)\). There are two objects that describe how \(f\) changes: its differential \(df\) and its gradient \(\nabla f\). If you have ever taken a calculus course, you might tell me that these are the same thing written in two ways: \(df = 2x \: dx + 2y \: dy\) and \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y)\). The coefficients are indeed identical. But this is a coincidence of the setting we are working in: the two objects are actually of a different nature.

The differential \(df\) is a machine that eats a direction and returns a number. Given a vector \(v = (v_x, v_y)\), it outputs \(df(v) = 2x\,v_x + 2y\,v_y\), the rate at which \(f\) changes when you move in the direction \(v\). To do its job, \(df\) needs no knowledge of lengths or angles; it only needs to know how \(f\) varies. Think of the contour lines on a hiking map: they contain everything there is to know about \(df\).

The gradient \(\nabla f = 2x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + 2y \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\) is a different kind of object: it is an arrow (a vector living in the tangent space) pointing in the direction of steepest ascent. And "steepest" hides a comparison: among all steps of the same length, which one makes you climb the most? To answer that question, you need to know what "the same length" means. (Equivalently, the gradient is perpendicular to the contour lines, and "perpendicular" is a statement about angles.) The rule that tells you how to measure lengths and angles is called a metric.

Here is a simple example. Imagine a map drawn on a stretched grid: one unit to the east is 1 km, but one unit to the north is 2 km. Take the simplest possible landscape, \(f(x,y) = x + y\). Moving one unit east or one unit north raises the altitude by the same amount, so \(df = dx + dy\), whatever the grid looks like: the contour lines don't care how we measure distances. But walk one kilometre in each direction and you gain \(1\) going east, but only \(\frac{1}{2}\) going north. The steepest way up therefore tilts towards the east, and with this metric \(\nabla f = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\) instead of \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\) (one factor \(\frac{1}{2}\) because northward steps are less rewarding, another because the arrow \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\) is itself 2 km long). Same landscape, same differential, different gradient. So where was the metric hiding in your calculus course? The metric's job is to convert an arrow into a measuring machine and back, and the gradient is precisely the arrow that converts into \(df\). With the usual Euclidean metric (the dot product), this conversion leaves the coefficients unchanged, which is why \(df\) and \(\nabla f\) look identical in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). On a curved space, where the metric varies from point to point, they can look very different, the differential does not depend on the metric, but the gradient does.

This is why we use differential forms, they describe derivatives intrinsically, that is to say, without assuming a metric. I will not give a rigorous definition of differential forms, because this would require introducing something called the exterior algebra (which really is the correct language for describing areas, volumes and their higher-dimensional analogues). I will simply try to convey a good idea of what they are.

As it turns out, we have already met a \(1\)-(differential) form, the differential \(df\) above (surprise!). Recall that it takes one vector and returns a number. But maybe we don't want to measure along directions anymore, maybe we want to measure areas or volumes. This is what the \(2\)-form \(dx \wedge dy\) and the \(3\)-form \(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\) do. The symbol \(\wedge\) is called the exterior product, and it is what allows us to combine directions into areas, areas into volumes, and so on... For our purposes, this will be enough to remeber.

The last operation on differential forms I want to introduce is the exterior derivative.
It is denoted \(d : k\text{-forms} \rightarrow (k+1)\text{-forms}\), and it is really the generalisation of differentiation we were looking for. Here is how it works:

  • For a function \(f(x,y)\) (a \(0\)-form), it simply outputs its differential \(df\) (a \(1\)-form), which in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) we can identify with the gradient using the Euclidean metric, as above.
  • For a \(1\)-form \(\alpha = f(x,y)\,dx + g(x,y)\,dy\), the exterior derivative gives the \(2\)-form \(d\alpha = \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) dx \wedge dy\). Concretely, it measures how much \(\alpha\) "swirls" around each point: go around a tiny loop, add up what \(\alpha\) measures along the way, and divide by the area of the loop. This can be identified with the curl \(\nabla \times F\) (in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)). PICTURE
  • For a \(2\)-form in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), \(\beta = p(x,y,z)\, dy \wedge dz + q(x,y,z)\, dz \wedge dx + r(x,y,z)\, dx \wedge dy\), the exterior derivative returns the \(3\)-form \(d\beta = \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial r}{\partial z}\right) dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\). It measures how much "flow" escapes from a tiny box, divided by its volume, and it can be identified with the divergence.
  • And so forth...
And finally, the exterior derivative has the property that, applying it twice always gives zero, \(d(d\omega) = 0\) for every form \(\omega\) (we will return to this later on).

What does (co)homology mean? (such a weird name right)
First of all, there are two notions here, homology and cohomology. And they are actually two sides of the same coin, with many "iterations" of it, a little bit like there are mutiple currencies that use coins. Concretely, homology is the study of holes. It gives us a way to define what a hole is and it allows us to distinguish between different kinds of holes and how to find these holes.

Now, you may argue that you already know what a hole is! For example, if you do a cut inside a slice of paper, well, you have a hole. And you'd be half right. The issue here is that we want to understand every kind of hole, so higher dimensional holes as well. I will not explain what it means to have a \(0,1,2, ...\)-dimensional hole, but I will give you two examples in which we will see how we can detect holes.

Imagine you have a sphere, a hollowed ball if you will. What we can do to test for the existence of holes on that sphere is to consider a loop on its surface (like putting a rubber band arround a huge ping-pong ball) and move it arround. If you assume that your rubber band is magic and that it can shrink so small until it becomes a point, then this situation will actually happen here. Without lifting the ruber band from the ping-poing ball, by sliding all of it to one part of the sphere, you can make it shrink to a single point ! (look at the picture) This exactly means that the sphere does not have a \(1\)-dimensional hole.

Now in place of a sphere, consider a torus (or an inflated swim ring), and attach a rubber band to it. The situation here is a bit different, there is actually two ways in which you can fit a rubber band onto a torus; one is to just put your rubber band onto the "exterior" of the torus and the other one is to imagine that the inflatable swim ring was constructed with a rubber band on its "inside" part (do you get it from picture bellow?). And this is a completely different situation than the one we had with the sphere. For the torus, it is impossible to shrink either rubber bands into a single point without lifting and breaking them (you can try if you want!). Informally, this means that the torus has two \(1\)-dimensional holes. And this actually tells us a lot about the structure of the inflatable swim ring, it hints to us the fact that the torus is "made of" two circles (mathematically, we write \(\mathbb{T}\cong S^1 \times S^1\)).

So far what we have checked for \(1\)-dimensional holes, but what about the hollowness of the sphere and the torus? Are they holes as well? How do we detect that?

Well, there is a way to describe and generalise this processus formally. The idea is to use chains, cycles and boundaries. Suppose we have a (resonnable topological) space \(X\) (think of a ping-pong ball, an inflatable swim ring, even spacetime! or whatever smooth space you like). The alternative for loops we use here are called \(k\)-chains. They can be thought of as a set of \(k\)-dimensional edges (with a direction), that you can attach together (at its endpoints). A \(0\)-chain can be thought of as a point, a \(1\)-chain may be something like a sequence of arrows (a directed graph if you will), a \(2\)-chain a set of planes attached to one another and so one... If the sequence of element in the \(k\)-chain meet at its endpoints, then this forms a loop! And surprise surprise, we can put loops on our objects and check for holes!

Now the correct notion of loops we want to use here are called cycles. And cycles are detected using the boundary, which records where your chains ends positively and where it starts negatively. Remember their endpoints is where we attach our \(k\)-elements to make a full \(k/)-chain, so when "added up", if the boundary sums to zero, we have a cycle.

Now I will repeat everything but with the correct wording. Let \(C_k(X)\) be a set whose elements \(\omega\) are \(k\)-chains. And let \(\partial_i: C_i(X) \rightarrow C_{i-1}(X)\) be a rule sending an \(i\)-chain to its boundary (look at the PICTURE for intuition about what boundaries are). We also require an extra condition for \(\partial\); applying the rule two consecutive times cancels everything (\(\partial_{k-1} \circ \partial_k = 0\)), this seems familiar right ? Notice that the boundary of an \(i+1\)-form is also an \(i\)-cycle (by applying twice annulates thing), so \(\text{im}(d_{i+1})\subset \text{ker}(d_i)\). Finally we can define what the \(n\)-th homology group of \(X\) written \(H_n(X)\) is, $$ H_n(X) = \frac{\omega \mid \partial_n\omega = 0}{\omega \mid \partial_{n+1}\eta = \omega}.$$ Now, \(H_n(X)\) is not only a group, but also a vector space, and dim \(H_n(X)\) is exactly the number of \(n\)-dimensional holes of the space \(X\).

Now cohomolgy on the other hand asks questions about functions (this is usually how we think about objects dually). Instead of asking what cycles does the space contain, we ask what functions/rules can be put on those cycles and what are they telling us about our space ? So for example instead of defining a \(k\)-chain as above, we will have a \(k\)-cochain which is a function \(f:C_k(X) \rightarrow R\) (where \(R\) is some number set, usually a ring).
Something that happens when taking working dually is that we have to reverse the arrows (this way of thinking comes from category theory, and in its language this translates to: the dual operator is actually a contravariant functor). What I mean is that our coboundary operator \(\delta^i\) takes an \(i-1\)-cochain in \(C^{i-1}\) and sends it a an \(i\)-cochain in \(C^{i}\). A useful practice people often do in algebraic topology and category theory is draw diagrams.

We have thus defined cochains and a coboundary operator. So like before, we can now define cocycles and hence the cohomology of a space \(X\), written $$ H^n(X) := \frac{\alpha \mid \delta^n\alpha = 0}{\alpha \mid \delta^n\beta = \alpha}. $$

Now, one of the many currencies that exist in cohomolgy is the de Rham cohomology. This is where differential forms and cohomology theory merge. In place of taking cochains as abstract objets, we say that \(\Omega^i(X)\) is the set of \(i\)-differential forms on \(X\) and that the boundary operator \(d\) is the exterior derivative.
