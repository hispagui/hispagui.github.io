A peculiar interpretation of derivatives

The motivation for this article comes from William Thurston's superb essay On proof and progress in mathematics. In this discussion I will explore deep and intricate connections from already hard mathematical concetps. My goal is to provide the best possible intuition for those ideas. In this context, I will resist my urge for using technical language and do my best to keep the discussion light and visual. But, for sake of understanding, I must introduce two important abstract concepts, that of differential forms and (co)homology.

As the title suggests, in this essay we are going to discuss about derivatives. You have certainly encountered derivatives before and you may even be very familiar with these objects. However, there is an important caveat here. You may know how to differentiate functions that live in Euclidean spaces, but how do you differentiate a function over, say a Klein bottle (which has no inside, nor outside and no boundary) ? This might be strange to consider at first, but this distinction is very important! Mathematicians and physicists frequently work over curved, hyperbolic, ..., whatever weird spaces all the time!
For example, imagine you want to know the speed of a probe that you have sent into space. Well this probe now lives in a curved space (spacetime) and you have to take into account the curvature of spacetime in your calcultions.

The question now becomes how do you measure stuff on weird spaces ? How can I make sense of what angles and distances are on a hyperbolic space for example ? The answer is differential forms! Essentially, they are used to measure something geometric. Let me show you concretely what we want to do here.

Take the gradient \( \nabla f \) and the differential \(df\) of a function \(f\), and say \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\).
On one hand, \(df = 2x \: dx + 2y \: dy\), it eats a vector \(v = (v_x,v_y)\) and outputs \(df(v) = 2xv_x + 2yv_y\), the derivative of \(f\) at \((x,y)\) in the direction \(v\). On the other hand, \(\nabla f = 2x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + 2y \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\) (it lives in the tangent space).
Now if you have ever taken a calculus course you would have encountered such notations and you would think; ok, I know that \(dx\)'s keep track of the variables with respect to which I am differentiating and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\)'s are the basis vectors of the tangent space. And this is a reasonable explanation, your life would be perfectly fine ... if you are not a mathematician.

The subtelty hides in the relation between \(dx\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\). Maybe you have heard that they are dual basis elements; \(dx (\frac{\partial}{\partial x}) = 1, dx (\frac{\partial}{\partial y}) = 0, dy (\frac{\partial}{\partial x})=0\) and \(dy (\frac{\partial}{\partial y}) = 1\) which is true in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). The secret here is that, we are using the Euclidean metric, this maps \(\frac{\partial}{\partial i}\) to \(dj\) with a factor \(\delta^i_j\) (\(1\) if \(i=j\) and \(0\) otherwise). But what if the space is curved and weird and highly dimensional ? Then the metric will also be weird, we may not have a factor \(1\) or \(0\), it may be a function depending on position.

Then we use differential forms, because they describe derivatives intrinsically, that is to say, without assuming a metric. I will not give a rigorous definition of differential forms because this would require the introduction of something called the exterior algebra (which really is the correct language for descrbing areas, volumes, and their higher-dimensional analogues). I will simply try to convey a good idea of what they are.
As it stands, we have already encountered a \(1\)-(differential) form! This was the differential \(df\) we have introduced above (surpise !), recall it takes a vector and returns a number. Now we want to generalise this process to higher dimensional things. Maybe we don't want to measure lengths anymore but areas or volumes. This is what a \(2\)-form \( dx \wedge dy \) and \(3\)-form \( dx \wedge dy \wedge dz \) do for example. The symbol \(\wedge\) is called the exterior product, it is what allows us to combine directions into areas, areas into volumes and so on... giving us \(k\)-forms.
SUMMARY 0,1,2,3-forms.
This is where it might start skipping a few things, it really is enough for us to just remember that the exterior product is used to combine lower dimensional things to measure into higher dimensional things to measure.

The last operator on differential forms I want to introduce is the exterior derivative. It is denoted \(d : k\text{-forms} \rightarrow (k+1)\text{-forms}\) and it is really the generalisation of the differentiation we wanted. How it works is as follow;
For a function \(f(x,y)\) (a \(O\)-form) it simply outputs its differential \(df\) (a \(1\)-form) which in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) we can identify with the gradient via the metric as above.
For a \(1\)-form \(\alpha = f(x,y)dx + g(x,y)dy \), the exterior derivative gives \(d\alpha = (\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}) dx \wedge dy \) (a \(2\)-form). concretely, it measures how. In turn, this can be identified with the curl (\(\nabla \times F\)) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\).
For a \(2\)-form \(\beta = f(x,y,z) dx \wedge dy + g(x,y,z) dy \wedge dz + h(x,y,z) dx \wedge dz \), the exterior derivative returns a \(3\)-form that can be identified with the divergence in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). And so forth...
Finally, the exterior derivative has the property of being a nilpotent, that is, applying it twice cancels evrything. You don't have to understand how all the calculations work, but just the idea behind what it does.

Well, what does (co)homology even mean (such a weird name right)?
First of all, there are two notions here, homology and cohomology. And they are actually two sides of the same coin, with many flavours to it, a little bit like there are mutiple currencies that use coins.
Concretely, homology is the study of holes.
It gives us a way to define what a hole is and it allows us to distinguish between different kinds of holes and how to find these holes.
Now, you may argue that you already know what a hole is! For example, if you do a cut inside a slice of paper, well, you have a hole. And you'd be half right. The issue here is that we want to understand every kind of hole, so higher dimensional holes as well.
I will not explain what it means to have a \(0,1,2, ...\)-dimensional hole, but I will give you two examples in which I will explain how we can detect a hole.
Imagine you have a sphere, a hollowed ball if you will. What we can do to test for the existence of holes on that sphere is to consider a loop on its surface (like putting a rubber band arround a huge ping-pong ball) and move it arround. If you assume that your rubber band is magic and that it can shrink so small until it becomes a point, then this situation will actually happen here. Without lifting the ruber band from the ping-poing ball, by sliding all of it to one part of the sphere, you can make it shrink to a single point ! (look at the picture) This exactly means that the sphere does not have a \(1\)-dimensional hole.
Now in place of a sphere, consider a torus (or an inflated swim ring), and attach a rubber band to it. The situation here is a bit different, there is actually two ways in which you can fit a rubber band onto a torus; one is to just put your rubber band onto the "exterior" of the torus and the other one is to imagine that the inflatable swim ring was constructed with a rubber band on its "inside" part (do you get it from picture bellow?). And this is a completely different situation than the one we had with the sphere. For the torus, it is impossible to shrink either rubber bands into a single point without lifting and breaking them (you can try if you want!). This means that the torus has two \(1\)-dimensional holes. And this actually tells us a lot about the structure of the inflatable swim ring, it hints to us the fact that the torus has two circles in it (in mathematical, we usually write \(\mathbb{T}\cong S^1 \times S^1 \)).
So far what we have checked for \(1\)-dimensional holes, but what about the hollowness of the sphere and the torus? Are they holes as well? How do we detect that?

Well, there is a way to describe and generalise this processus formally. The idea is to use chains, cycles and boundaries. Suppose we have a (resonnable topological) space \(X\) (think of a ping-pong ball, an inflatable swim ring, even spacetime! or whatever smooth space you like).
The alternative for loops we use here are called \(k\)-chains. They can be thought of as a set of \(k\)-dimensional edges, with a direction, that you can attach together (at its endpoints). A \(0\)-chain can be thought of as a point, a \(1\)-chain may be something like a sequence of arrows (a directed graph if you will), a \(2\)-chain a set of planes attached to one another and so one...
If the sequence of element in the \(k\)-chain meet at its endpoints, then this forms a loop! And surprise surprise, we can put loops on our objects and check for holes!
PICTURE
Now the correct notion of loops we want to use here are cycles. And cycles are detected using the boundary, recording where you end, positively and where you start, negatively. (ok there is a little trick here, we can use algebra to sum elements in the chains, and when the elements of the boundary sum to 0, we have a cycle)
Now I will repeat everything but with the correct notations.
Let \(C_k(X)\) be the set whose elements are \(k\)-chains. And let \(\partial_i: C_i(X) \rightarrow C_{i-1}(X)\) be a rule sending an \(i\)-chain to its boundary (look at the picture for intuition about what boundaries are). We also require an extra condition for \(\partial\); applying the rule two consecutive times cancels everything (\(\partial_{k-1} \circ \partial_k = 0\)).
Formally, we have the following sequence, $$ \{0\text{-chains}\} \xleftarrow{\partial_0} \{1\text{-chains}\} \xleftarrow{\partial_1} \{2\text{-chains}\} \xleftarrow{\partial_2} \{3\text{-chains}\} \xleftarrow{\partial_3} ... $$ Let (so \(i\)-chains whose endpoints meet) and \(B_i := \text{im}(d_{i+1})\) the set of boundaries. Notice that an \(i+1\) boundary is also a \(i\)-cycle, so \(\text{im}(d_{i+1})\subset \text{ker}(d_i)\). Finally we can define what the \(n\)-th homology group of \(X\) written \(H_n(X)\), it is
$$ H_n(X) = \frac{\omega \mid \partial_n\omega = 0}{\omega \mid \partial_n\eta = \omega}. $$ It is important noting that it is also a vector space and dim \(H_n(X)\) is exactly the number of \(n\)-dimensional holes of the space \(X\). Now cohomolgy on the other hand asks questions about functions (this is usually how we think about objects dually). Instead of asking what cycles does the space contain, we ask what functions/rules can be put on those cycles ??? -such that- that detect their topology??? So for example instead of defining a \(k\)-chain as above, we will have a \(k\)-cochain which is a function \(f:C_k(X) \rightarrow R\) (where \(R\) is some number set, usually a ring). Something that happens when taking the duals is that we have to reverse the arrows (this way of thinking comes from category theory, and in this language this translates to: the dual operator is actually a contravariant functor). What I mean is that our coboundary operator \(\delta^i\) takes an \(i\)-cochain in \(C^i\) and sends it a an \(i+1\)-cochain in \(C^{i+1}\). What people often do in algebraic topology and category theory is draw diagrams. This is very useful when we want to visualise what we are talking about.

We have thus defined cochains and a coboundary operator, as above, we can now define cocycles and hence the cohomology of a space \(X\), written $$ H^n(X) := \frac{\alpha \mid \delta^n\alpha = 0}{\alpha \mid \delta^n\beta = \alpha}. $$

Now, one of the many currencies that exist in cohomolgy is the de Rham cohomology. This is where differential forms and cohomology theory merge.
In place of taking cochains as abstract objets, we say that \(\Omega^i(X)\) is the set of \(i\)-differential forms on a smooth manifold \(X\) and that the boundary operator \(d\) is the exterior derivative.
